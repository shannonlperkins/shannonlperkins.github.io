The Business Problem

The practice appears busy and highly utilized, yet new-patient access continues to deteriorate. The operational question is whether the true constraint is total provider capacity—or how available capacity is allocated and supported.

The model shows a structural deficit of 19 new-patient appointments per week: 78 requests versus 59 dedicated slots. At the same time, weekly scheduling demand is approximately 1,450 calls, while three scheduling FTE can handle about 1,260.

That creates two linked bottlenecks: insufficient new-patient appointment allocation and insufficient front-end scheduling capacity.

Decision question
How can leadership improve new-patient access while preserving established-patient availability and avoiding the creation of a second bottleneck in scheduling operations?

How I Structured the Analysis

Rather than treating access as a single productivity metric, the model evaluates interdependent clinical and administrative constraints.

Provider SupplyClinical FTE, days per week, slots per day, template utilization, no-show assumptions, and dedicated new-patient allocation.
Patient DemandWeekly new-patient requests, established-patient demand, starting backlog, and 12-week backlog projection.
Access OperationsScheduling call volume, staffing capacity, scenario changes, and whether patients can realistically reach available inventory.

Key Findings

The model changes the management question from “How do we increase productivity?” to “Where is the system actually constrained?”

Finding 01

The primary constraint is capacity allocation.

Overall established-patient supply remains adequate in the model, while dedicated new-patient capacity falls short. The first intervention should therefore test template redesign before assuming the practice needs a full additional clinical FTE.

Finding 02

Template rebalancing helps, but does not fully solve access.

Reallocating 14 weekly slots toward new patients materially reduces the deficit, but demand still exceeds new-patient supply. The backlog grows more slowly rather than reversing.

Finding 03

Clinical expansion alone can leave a hidden bottleneck.

Evening sessions and additional APP capacity create more appointment inventory, but the baseline scheduling team still lacks enough call-handling capacity to absorb demand efficiently.

Finding 04

The strongest scenario addresses both sides of access.

The recommended combination improves appointment allocation and front-end scheduling capacity, allowing modeled new-patient supply to exceed demand while preserving established-patient capacity above modeled demand.

Recommended Operating Strategy

Use the lowest-cost structural changes first, add targeted capacity where the model shows it is needed, and expand front-end support at the same time so additional appointment supply is actually accessible to patients.

  • Rebalance templates before adding full clinical FTE. Shift available established-patient capacity only where demand remains safely covered.
  • Add targeted evening access. Create incremental supply without immediately committing to broad schedule expansion.
  • Add 0.5 scheduling FTE. Bring call-handling capacity closer to weekly demand as appointment inventory grows.
  • Manage access as a system. Review demand, supply, backlog, call volume, template utilization, no-shows, and established-patient capacity together.

Leadership Recommendations

The model is designed to support operational decisions, not just produce a dashboard.

1. Test allocation before expansion.Determine whether existing capacity can be redistributed before committing to more expensive clinical staffing.
2. Use targeted interventions.Add access sessions where demand is concentrated instead of expanding all schedules uniformly.
3. Match administrative capacity to clinical capacity.Do not create appointment inventory that patients cannot efficiently reach or book.
4. Establish intervention thresholds.Define triggers for backlog, demand-capacity gaps, call overload, and established-patient capacity risk.
