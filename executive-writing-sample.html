Improving Ambulatory Access Through Capacity and Template Optimization

A concise executive recommendation memo demonstrating how I translate operating data into a leadership decision, implementation path, and measures of success.

Executive CommunicationPatient AccessCapacity PlanningImplementation
To: Executive Leadership
From: Shannon Perkins, MBA
Subject: Improving new-patient access without broad clinical expansion

Executive Recommendation

The modeled practice has an access problem, but the data do not support treating it as a simple provider-productivity problem. Weekly new-patient demand is 78 requests against 59 dedicated new-patient slots, creating a structural deficit of 19 appointments per week. The practice also begins with a 105-patient backlog. At the same time, scheduling operations receive approximately 1,450 calls per week while three scheduling FTE can handle about 1,260.

Recommendation: Rebalance existing appointment templates toward new-patient access, add targeted evening access sessions, and add 0.5 scheduling FTE. This addresses both appointment supply and the administrative capacity required to convert that supply into actual patient access. In the 12-week model, the combined scenario reverses backlog growth while preserving established-patient capacity above modeled demand.

Why the Current Model Underperforms

The central issue is capacity allocation. Overall provider supply is not uniformly constrained, but too little of that supply is reserved for new patients. Maintaining the existing templates while asking providers to work harder would not correct the underlying mismatch.

A template-rebalance scenario shifts 14 weekly slots toward new patients without adding total provider capacity. That materially improves the gap, but it does not fully close it. Demand still exceeds supply, so the backlog continues to grow—just more slowly.

The second constraint sits upstream of the visit itself. Adding appointments without increasing scheduling capacity can create inventory that patients still struggle to reach. With call demand exceeding modeled handling capacity by approximately 190 calls per week, clinical expansion alone risks moving the bottleneck rather than solving it.

Recommended Operating Strategy

Rebalance templates before adding full clinical FTE.

Use provider- and session-level capacity data to identify where established-patient supply can be shifted toward new-patient visits while keeping established access above demand.

Add targeted evening access instead of broad schedule expansion.

Create incremental new-patient supply in defined sessions where demand is concentrated. This adds capacity without immediately committing to a full additional provider or uniformly extending every schedule.

Increase front-end scheduling capacity by 0.5 FTE.

Align call-handling capacity with the additional appointment inventory. Access should be managed as an end-to-end system: appointment supply, booking capacity, backlog, and patient demand must move together.

Implementation Approach

First 30 days — validate the operating baseline.

Confirm weekly new-patient demand, dedicated new-patient slots, established-patient demand, utilization, no-show rates, backlog, call volume, and calls handled per scheduling FTE. Identify providers or sessions with rebalancing opportunity.

Days 31–60 — pilot the changes.

Shift selected template capacity, launch limited evening access sessions, and deploy the additional scheduling support. Use a defined pilot group rather than changing every provider template at once.

Days 61–90 — evaluate and standardize.

Compare actual demand, backlog movement, established-patient access, call capacity, utilization, and no-shows with baseline. Retain changes that improve access without creating a new constraint; revise or stop those that do not.

Measures of Success

Leadership should review a small set of linked indicators rather than a single productivity metric: weekly new-patient demand versus capacity, backlog size and direction, projected access days, established-patient capacity versus demand, scheduling call demand versus handling capacity, template utilization, and no-show rate.

The most important signal is not simply whether more appointments are created. It is whether the system consistently produces enough usable new-patient capacity to meet demand while protecting established access and keeping scheduling workload within manageable limits.

Leadership Takeaway

The modeled access challenge is a system-design problem, not a call for across-the-board productivity pressure. The lowest-risk path is to reallocate existing capacity first, add targeted supply where the gap remains, and strengthen scheduling support at the same time. This sequencing creates a more disciplined alternative to adding broad clinical FTE before the organization has proven that its current capacity is being allocated and accessed effectively.

Portfolio note: All volumes, staffing assumptions, scenarios, and data in this memo are synthetic and were created solely for professional portfolio demonstration. No patient information, PHI, employer data, or proprietary organizational information is included.
